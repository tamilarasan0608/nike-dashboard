03
The Three Strategic Frameworks
Why Nike's approach was fundamentally flawed — and what should have happened
[ 01 ] Framework 1: Kraljic Matrix — Supply Risk × Profit Impact

What is it? 2×2 matrix classifying products by profit impact (low/high) and supply risk (low/high). Four categories: Non-Critical (buy cheapest), Leverage (negotiate hard), Bottleneck (secure supply), Strategic (invest in relationships).

Nike's Fatal Mistake: AF1 is STRATEGIC ($2.8B revenue + 60% Vietnam concentration = high profit impact + high supply risk). But Nike treated it as LEVERAGE — pure commodity sourcing, aggressive cost cutting, zero relationship investment.

Why it Failed: Strategic products require diversified suppliers, relationship investment, and resilience buffers. Leverage products can be cost-optimised with no consequences. By misclassifying AF1, Nike destroyed its own resilience.

The Correct Action (2022): Recognise AF1 as Strategic → Begin diversification to Indonesia/India → Avoid crisis entirely at ~$50M cost instead of $357M crisis cost.

Source: Kraljic, P. (1983). "Purchasing must become supply management." Harvard Business Review.

[ 02 ] Framework 2: Total Cost of Ownership (TCO)

What is it? Calculates FULL cost, not just purchase price. Includes: base price, quality control, logistics, supply chain risk, geopolitical exposure, opportunity costs.

Nike's Mistake: Only counted direct price ($14.20/pair). Ignored tariff scenarios, geopolitical risk premium, demand elasticity, and concentration risk.

Real TCO Breakdown:

  • Manufacturing price: $14.20 (labor + materials)
  • Quality control: $0.50 / Logistics: $1.20
  • Supply risk premium (ignored by Nike): $X
  • Real TCO before tariff: ~$16.45/pair
  • With 46% tariff applied: $23.98/pair (+46%)

TCO Comparison (2023, if modelled correctly): Vietnam full TCO $23.98 vs Indonesia stable TCO $17.80 → Diversification was obviously superior. Nike never ran this model.

Source: Analysis based on Nike 10-K filings (FY2023-2025) and supply chain cost structures.

[ 03 ] Framework 3: Supply Chain Risk Management (SCRM)

What is it? Risk = Probability × Impact × Detectability. Identifies vulnerabilities and probability-weights expected losses to prioritise mitigation.

Nike's Mistake: Assigned ~10% probability to Vietnam tariff scenario. Evidence by 2023 suggested 35-50% was correct.

The Math — How It Should Have Been Calculated:

  • At Nike's 10%: Risk = $117.5M × 0.1 = $11.7M (seemed acceptable — no action)
  • At correct 35%: Risk = $117.5M × 0.35 = $41M (action required)
  • At realistic 50%: Risk = $117.5M × 0.5 = $58.75M (critical — diversify now)

Evidence the probability was higher (2022-2023):

  • U.S.-China trade war ongoing (direct precedent for tariffs)
  • Biden → Trump tariff escalation trajectory
  • Vietnam trade surplus with US growing every year
  • Vietnam concentration was publicly visible (Nike 10-K disclosed it)

Source: Christopher, M., & Peck, H. (2004). "Building the Resilient Supply Chain." International Journal of Logistics Management.

WHY — Four Strategic Drivers
  • Eliminate tariff exposure: Reducing Vietnam to 30% cuts annual tariff from $117.5M → ~$35M (70% reduction)
  • Maintain cost competitiveness: Indonesia + India blended avg ($15.10) barely higher than Vietnam baseline ($14.20), well below Vietnam + tariff ($20.73)

8,000 Workers → Other Nike Products

Nike's apparel, accessories, and equipment factories globally. Footwear skills (sewing, assembly) transfer directly to apparel manufacturing. Nike prefers to rehire displaced workers — retains training investment and loyalty. These workers transition to other Nike facilities in Vietnam or nearby.

7,000 Workers → Other Footwear Brands

Adidas, Puma, Skechers, Bata operate footwear factories in Vietnam. Vietnam footwear market has ~500K workers with 20-25% annual turnover = 100-125K openings/year. 7,000 = just 7% of annual openings (easily absorbed). Wage slightly lower than Nike (5-10% reduction).

5,000 Workers → Other Industries

Electronics assembly (250K+ workers Vietnam), textiles (200K+), automotive (50K+). Footwear assembly skills transfer to 60%+ of these roles. Wage may be 15-25% lower but stable employment available. Represents transition to non-footwear manufacturing in Vietnam's growing industrial base.

2,500 Workers → Agriculture / Home Production

Last-resort fallback for workers unable to find factory employment. Return to family farming or informal self-employment. $5K severance = 33 months agricultural income (at $150/month farm wage). Nike advocates for government agricultural support programs and microfinance for affected communities.
